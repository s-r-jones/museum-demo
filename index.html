68–66 million years ago

Triceratops horridus

A three-horned plant-eater nearly 30 feet long, it walked the floodplains of western North America in the final days of the dinosaurs.

Smithsonian, 1905

Meet Hatcher

The first Triceratops ever mounted in a museum was pieced together from ten different animals. A century later it was rebuilt from accurate casts and scanned.

Up close

A beak and a battery of teeth

A toothless beak snipped plants; dozens of cheek teeth sliced them like scissors, and new teeth replaced worn ones throughout its life.

Ice Age · until about 10,000 years ago

Woolly mammoth

Mammuthus primigenius evolved in Asia and crossed Arctic land bridges into North America, where it shared the landscape with the continent's first people.

USNM 23792

A skeleton of many

This mount is a composite, built from the bones of several individuals. It was recovered in Alaska in 1952 and joined the Smithsonian's Ice Age Hall in 1966.

Digitized

Scanned where it stands

Laser scanners captured the skeleton on exhibit, giving researchers a way to study giant bones that are too heavy to handle.

Permian · about 280 million years ago

Diplocaulus magnicornis

Older than the first dinosaurs, this amphibian lived in streams and ponds and is known for its striking boomerang-shaped head.

Still a mystery

What was the boomerang for?

Even this partial skull shows the swept-back horn. Scientists still debate its purpose: steering in water, defense, or display.
